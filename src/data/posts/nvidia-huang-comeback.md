Two stories about Jensen Huang are running at the same time this week, and they barely seem to describe the same person. In one, he is a former CEO sued over disclosures tied to a suspected Chinese spy, with a second workplace relationship now public, and Bessent saying Nvidia must look outside the company for its next leader. In the other, he is the founder employees voted overwhelmingly to bring back, the man Elon Musk signed a petition for, and — according to the Financial Times — someone already discussing a comeback with allies. Both stories are true at once. That is the story.

## The legal case: a spy, a lawsuit, and a second relationship

The legal thread is the one the English-language business press is leading with. Reuters reports that Nvidia and Huang have been sued over disclosures related to a suspected Chinese spy. The BBC, meanwhile, is asking the question at the center of it — "Who is Alice Huang, the Chinese businesswoman tied to Nvidia's CEO?" — and reports that the person at the center of the spy allegation left Megaspeed in 2025.

Then came the second blow: a Reuters exclusive, sourced to people familiar with the matter, that the former Nvidia CEO had a relationship with a second employee. Note what the suit is actually about. It is not about chips, margins, or market share. It is about what was disclosed and what was not — a governance case, not a product case. That distinction matters, because governance cases are the kind a board can act on quickly, and this board evidently did.

The official response so far points outward, not inward. Bessent says Nvidia must "go outside the company" to find a new CEO (Reuters). Read plainly, that is a vote of no confidence not just in Huang but in the entire internal succession bench: whoever leads Nvidia next, in this telling, should not come from the building Huang built.

Markets, for their part, are watching something else entirely. Reuters notes Asian shares falling before Nvidia earnings, with the Anthropic IPO also testing the tech rally. Earnings are the market's real verdict — the question investors are actually asking is whether any of this touches the business.

## The comeback campaign: 97%, a Musk signature, and a board under fire

Turn to the Chinese-language discussion and the temperature changes completely. On Zhihu, the top questions are not about the lawsuit at all. They are about the employee retention drive: workers launched a campaign to keep Huang, and an internal vote came back with over 97% wanting him back. The most upvoted framing of the question is disbelief — is his personal pull really that strong?

Then Musk entered the story. Zhihu users are debating his decision to sign a petition calling for Huang's reinstatement, and 36Kr reports Musk's attack on the board in blistering terms — asking, in effect, how people whose background is selling fast food and soda should be deciding the fate of a trillion-dollar AI empire. It is the kind of line that only makes sense if you believe the board, not the founder, is the problem.

The comeback talk is no longer just a petition. Reuters reports, citing the FT, that Huang has discussed a return as Nvidia CEO with allies. 36Kr's headlines have escalated in step: "Huang considers returning as Nvidia CEO", then "Huang plots a return to the Nvidia CEO throne". One 36Kr piece even argues he "suffered from listing too early" — the suggestion being that the public-company governance machine, not any single decision, is what broke the relationship between founder and firm.

The Zhihu discussion also shows how emotional the whole thing has become. Users are chewing over a New York Times piece — framed in the discussion as the claim that Huang has "shamed leather-jacket lovers" — and one question, darkly joking, asks about his "biological survival probability". That is not analysis. That is a crowd that has picked a side and is now processing the story as drama.

## What the split tells us

Here is what the cross-source picture actually shows: the same facts, two different protagonists. In the BBC and Reuters telling, the protagonist is the disclosure regime — the rules, the lawsuit, the succession process. In the Zhihu and 36Kr telling, the protagonist is the founder — his charisma, his employees' loyalty, the board's competence. Neither telling is wrong about its own facts. They disagree about which facts are the story.

That disagreement is worth taking seriously, because founder-CEO comebacks after a governance rupture are rare. Boards that oust a founder almost never re-hire him; the act of removal is itself the statement that the relationship is over. What makes this case unusual is the scale of the counter-pressure: a 97% internal vote is not a polite disagreement, it is a workforce telling the board it got the decision wrong, and a Musk signature turns an internal dispute into a public referendum.

Three things to watch. First, Nvidia earnings — the market's verdict on whether any of this touches the business. Second, whether the board's external search produces a name or stalls, which will tell you how committed it really is to the "go outside" line. Third, whether the FT's comeback talks turn into anything formal, or stay at the level of allies talking to allies.

The paradox is only a paradox if you assume a CEO is one thing. Huang is, this week, two things at once: a legal liability and the person almost everyone inside the building wants back. Whichever story wins will say less about Huang than about who gets to decide what a company is — its board, its employees, or its founder.
